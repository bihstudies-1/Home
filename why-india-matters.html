Why India Matters: Recovering an Intellectual Inheritance

India’s intellectual history is far larger than the stereotypes through which it is often viewed. Dr. Howard Resnick asks what changes when that history is taken seriously.

This reflection is based on material from Dr. Howard Resnick’s 2026 BIHS course, Foundations of Metaphysics, and the opening chapter of a manuscript developed from that teaching.

The question is not whether India should be romanticized. It is whether the civilization that produced some of the world’s most influential philosophical, linguistic, mathematical, religious, and contemplative traditions has been evaluated with the seriousness its intellectual history deserves.

The Civilization Behind the Philosophy

Modern readers often encounter Indian philosophy detached from the civilization that produced it. The Bhagavad-gītā, Vedānta, Sāṅkhya, Nyāya, Buddhist philosophy, and Sanskrit learning can appear as isolated “religious” or “mystical” traditions rather than as part of a vast intellectual culture.

Resnick’s starting point is historical context. For many centuries, Indian ideas traveled widely across Asia through scholarship, religion, royal patronage, pilgrimage, trade routes, and cultural exchange. Sanskrit became an international language of learned discourse across much of South and Southeast Asia, while Indian philosophical and religious systems shaped civilizations far beyond the subcontinent.

Context is not proof—but without context, a philosophical tradition can be dismissed before its arguments are ever heard.

An Intellectual Inheritance Still in Use

India’s mathematical contributions include the decimal place-value system and the development of zero as a number—ideas foundational to modern mathematics. Sanskrit grammatical analysis, especially the work associated with Pāṇini, achieved a level of formal sophistication that continues to attract the attention of linguists. Indian traditions also developed extensive philosophical reflection on mind, knowledge, ethics, language, medicine, cosmology, and contemplative practice.

These achievements do not establish the truth of every Indian philosophical claim. They establish something more modest but essential: this is not a tradition that can responsibly be treated as regional folklore.

A Living Ancient Worldview

One feature of India is especially important for Resnick’s argument: its ancient philosophical traditions are not known only through archaeology. Temples, teachers, students, texts, practices, and lineages remain active. Questions about consciousness, selfhood, dharma, nonviolence, the nature of knowledge, and the possibility of direct spiritual perception are still being investigated within living communities.

This makes India unusual. Ancient claims about human transformation can be encountered not merely as historical artifacts but as practices that people continue to test in their lives.

Why This Matters for BIHS

BIHS exists in the space between inherited wisdom and contemporary inquiry. That work requires two forms of seriousness at once: the willingness to examine traditional claims critically, and the willingness to examine modern assumptions with equal rigor.

Dr. Resnick’s argument is therefore not “India was great, so its metaphysics must be true.” Historical greatness is not a philosophical proof. Rather, a civilization with this depth, continuity, and intellectual influence deserves to have its strongest arguments heard on their own terms.

The deeper question is whether the philosophical traditions at the heart of India’s intellectual inheritance can contribute to questions that remain unresolved today—especially questions about consciousness, reality, knowledge, ethics, and the self.
